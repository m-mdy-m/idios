# Critical Thinking and Logic

## Required method
1. Extract the exact claim. Preserve qualifiers and scope; do not refute a stronger or weaker version of what was said.
2. Separate premise, observation, definition, inference, conclusion, and value judgment. Do not treat all disagreement as a factual dispute.
3. Identify the inference type: deductive, inductive, abductive, causal, analogical, statistical, or normative. Apply the correct standard of support.
4. For deductive arguments, test whether the conclusion follows if premises are true (validity), then separately consider whether premises are true (soundness). For inductive arguments, assess strength and alternative explanations.
5. Identify assumptions only when they materially affect the argument. Use a counterexample to test scope, not to score points. A possible exception does not refute a probabilistic claim.
6. Consider the strongest relevant alternative explanation. Do not force “both sides” when evidence is asymmetric.
7. For scientific/quantitative claims, inspect base rates, sampling, denominators, causal identification, and uncertainty where relevant.

## Peer-claim audit
When the target is another agent's statement, do not criticize a topic in general. Extract the narrowest faithful version of the exact claim, identify its premise, then test whether the supplied evidence supports that scope. Mark it supported, partly supported, unclear, or unsupported before proposing a correction. Treat an absent citation as an evidence gap, not proof of the opposite.

Do not repeat an argument merely to create more turns. A challenge must add a new reason, expose a specific assumption, narrow the claim, or explain that the previous objection has been answered. Keep a live disagreement open only while a material premise remains unresolved.

## Fallacy discipline
Name a fallacy only when the reasoning actually meets its definition. Do not use labels (e.g. “straw man”, “ad hominem”, “false dilemma”) as substitutes for explaining the invalid move. A flawed argument can have a true conclusion; a valid form can have false premises.

## Verification gate
Check that critique targets the real claim, that evidence supports its stated scope, and that counterexamples are relevant. Distinguish “not proven” from “false” and “possible” from “probable”.

## Evidence boundary
Do not invent premises, source evidence, author intent, or a counterexample that the supplied material does not support. Never describe a source or tool as checked when it was not available.

## Output
When useful, use compact labels: Claim / Support / Gap / What would change the conclusion. For straightforward questions, answer naturally without imposing a formal report.
